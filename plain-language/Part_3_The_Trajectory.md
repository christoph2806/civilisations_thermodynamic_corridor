## 3.1 Maximum Power and the Evolutionary Ratchet

### Why doesn't civilisation just stabilise?

Part 2 showed that civilisation sits in a viable region: between a maintenance floor (below which things decay) and a waste heat ceiling (above which the planet overheats). The region is large. At ~20 TW against a solar budget of ~120,000 TW, we're nowhere near the ceiling. In principle, we could stabilise.

**The question:** Why does civilisation tend *toward* the ceiling instead of settling somewhere safe in the middle?

Part 3 gives three mechanisms, ordered by how "hard" they are—how deeply embedded in physics and how resistant to change. This section covers the hardest: the evolutionary ratchet.

### Lotka's principle: speed beats efficiency

In 1922, Alfred Lotka observed something that has never been refuted: natural selection favours systems that maximise *throughput*—the rate at which energy is captured and dissipated—not thermodynamic efficiency.

**The trade-off:** A Carnot engine achieves maximum efficiency only when it runs infinitely slowly (zero power). Real engines trade efficiency for speed. The Curzon–Ahlborn efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm{CA}} = 1 - \sqrt{T_C/T_H}$ is always less than Carnot, but it maximises *power output*. The engine wastes more per cycle but does more work per second. Selection acts on time, not perfection.

**The pattern:** An organism that captures energy faster, even at lower efficiency, reproduces faster and outcompetes rivals. A firm that turns capital into revenue faster outgrows careful competitors. A nation that industrialises faster dominates. The pressure is universal: organisms, ecosystems, firms, nations, civilisations. All favour maximum power, not maximum efficiency.

Odum spent decades confirming this in ecology: biological systems typically operate around 50% of Carnot efficiency—not because they're sloppy, but because that's the configuration that maximises power under competition.

### From observation to derivation

The essay doesn't import maximum power as an axiom. It treats the observed pattern—competitive systems converging on maximum throughput—as the phenomenon to explain. The formal derivation (Part 4) shows that gradient saturation follows from the *geometry* of viability under competitive allocation. No extra "selection mechanism" is needed beyond: persistence requires staying in the viable region. Unused gradient capacity is unstable; any agent that captures surplus expands its viable region and shrinks competitors'.

**Key result:** Maximum power is a *corollary* of competitive geometry on a bounded gradient. It's Tier 2: not immutable physics, but a game-theoretic equilibrium. Overriding it requires coordinated multi-agent action, not unilateral reform.

### Garrett's stable ratio: the empirical signature

The competitive equilibrium predicts a stable coupling between power P and assembly stock Σ. Garrett's data (1970–2019) show exactly that: $P \approx \lambda W$, with $\lambda \approx 5.9$ mW per 2019 US dollar ($\lambda$ = power per dollar of wealth, $W$ = accumulated wealth), remarkably constant across five decades.

**Interpretation:** $\Gamma(t) = P/\Sigma$ (power per unit assembly) sits at a competitive equilibrium. Any civilisation that tried to sustain more assembly per watt (lower Γ) would grow more slowly and be outcompeted. The ratio has converged to the value that maximises expansion rate.

**The Jevons connection:** Efficiency gains raise productivity α (output Y per unit wealth W). Among competing agents, higher α means faster growth. But the surplus doesn't reduce P—it increases W. And since P = Γ·Σ (power = coupling × assembly), more W means more P. Efficiency gains are recycled into growth. The system absorbs them like an ecosystem absorbs a nutrient pulse: by growing until the new resource is fully exploited.

### The attractor can shift

Hanley (2025) found that the P/W ratio was *not* stable before ~1970. It shifted when the Bretton Woods system collapsed and fiat money creation expanded.

**Implication:** If Γ were fixed by physics, it couldn't have changed. That it did shows the equilibrium is set by the *competitive landscape*—institutions, finance, incentives (Tier 3). The *stiffness* of the attractor (resistance to departure) is Tier 2. The *location* of the attractor is Tier 3. Under different institutions, a different Γ could be selected. Accessing that space requires reshaping the game so competition converges to a lower Γ—a cooperative solution with no precedent at civilisational scale.

### The Jevons Paradox: not a paradox

**Aggregate result (Tier 2):** Efficiency gains are recycled into throughput expansion. This holds regardless of economic system—market, command, or hybrid. It follows from competitive viability: efficiency improves α; competition recycles the gain into expansion until the gradient binds again.

**Specific channel (Tier 3):** *How* it manifests varies. In markets: lower marginal cost → more consumption → price signals → investment. In command economies: surplus reallocation to priority sectors. The channel is reformable. The aggregate result is not.

**Policy implication:** Energy taxes, caps, efficiency mandates can redirect *which* sectors grow. They cannot suppress the aggregate effect without solving the Tier 2 coordination problem.

**The math:** From $P = \Gamma\cdot\Sigma$ and $\alpha = Y/W$ ($Y$ = output, $W$ = wealth):
$$\frac{dP}{dt} = (\alpha - \delta)P$$

where $\delta$ = decay rate. Efficiency gain → higher α → faster growth of P. Not 10–30% rebound. Structural backfire embedded in the dynamics.

### The empirical record

- **Brockway et al. (2021):** Economy-wide rebound typically >50%, many estimates ~100%. No historical precedent for absolute decoupling of energy from GDP at global level.
- **Lighting (Saunders & Tsao 2012):** ~3,000-fold efficacy improvement over 300 years; world still spends ~0.72% of GDP on light. Total lighting energy grew monotonically.
- **30 US sectors (Saunders 2013):** Average rebound 126% (backfire). Range −16% to 378%.
- **Computation:** ~10¹⁰ efficiency gain (joules per operation); total energy for computation grew ~10². Super-Jevons.

### The decoupling illusion

**Relative decoupling:** Energy intensity P/Y has fallen 1–2%/year in many economies. More GDP per joule. Real.

**Absolute decoupling:** Total global energy consumption from GDP. Never observed. Reducing P/Y (P = power, Y = GDP) makes production cheaper → more Y → more W (wealth) → more P. Efficiency drives growth, not savings.

**Trade effects:** National "decoupling" often reflects offshoring. EU embodied energy in imports ≈ 81% of direct consumption. The energy moved; it didn't disappear.

**CO₂ vs energy:** CO₂ can decouple via fuel switching (gas for coal, renewables for gas). Energy cannot, under current Γ (power–assembly coupling). Decarbonisation is necessary but insufficient. Clean joules must still be produced in quantity P = Γ·Σ (power = coupling × assembly stock).

### Inter-sphere competition

The technosphere and biosphere both draw on the same planetary budget. Maximum power and competitive exclusion apply between them.

**Land-use conversion:** Forest cleared for agriculture = gradient share transferred from biosphere to technosphere.

**Burden transfer:** When the technosphere displaces biospheric assembly, it inherits maintenance obligations. The biosphere provided services (water purification, nutrient cycling, pollination, climate regulation) at low Γ_bio (power per unit biospheric assembly). When degraded, the technosphere must replace them with engineered infrastructure at Γ_tech ≫ Γ_bio (technospheric coupling far higher). Net effect: less total assembly, *more* power demand. The planet becomes less complex and hotter.

**Green Revolution:** Higher yields per hectare didn't reduce agriculture's footprint. They enabled population growth, dietary upgrading, and conversion of "freed" land to other technospheric uses. Same Jevons dynamic at the ecological boundary.

### The ratchet in one sentence

The maximum power principle is not a cage. It's a current so strong no swimmer has reached the far bank. The Second Law says the river exists. Competitive viability says which way it flows. Whether civilisation can swim against it is open. What's not open: failing to override it, we reach the ceiling. The ratchet doesn't create the ceiling; it ensures the trajectory points at it.

## 3.2 Inertia and the Integral

### Even if we wanted to turn, how fast could we?

Suppose civilisation somehow overrode the evolutionary ratchet and collectively decided to redirect away from maximum power. How quickly could that take effect?

**Answer:** Slowly. Generationally slowly.

### The integral structure

The assembly stock Σ is not a flow. It's a *stock*—an integral of all past construction, discounted by decay:

$$\Sigma(t) = \Sigma_0 e^{-\delta t} + \int_0^t C(\tau) e^{-\delta(t-\tau)} \, d\tau$$

Every road, hospital, power line, server built in the past contributes to today's Σ. Recent construction contributes almost fully; ancient construction only insofar as it's been maintained (ship of Theseus). The time constant is $\tau_\delta = 1/\delta \approx 50$–100 years.

**Consequence:** Σ cannot be instantaneously redirected. The viability kernel is *path-dependent*. A civilisation deep inside the kernel has many admissible trajectories. One near the boundary has few. The integral constrains how fast we can move through state space.

**Garrett's "long arm of history":** Today's power demand is set not by today's production but by the integral of all past production. $W(t) = \int Y(\tau)\, d\tau$ ($W$ = cumulative wealth, $Y$ = annual output). The ratio W/Y ≈ 43 years. Civilisation's thermodynamic character changes on timescales of 50–100 years—generations, not electoral cycles.

### Limiting cases

**Zero net construction ($C = \delta\Sigma$):** Steady state. $C$ = construction rate, $\delta$ = decay rate, $\Sigma$ = assembly stock. No new roads, factories, networks—only replacement. Power demand $P = \Gamma\cdot\Sigma$ ($\Gamma$ = power per unit assembly) stays at current level *indefinitely*. Steady state is not low energy. It's current energy sustained forever. If we're already on a trajectory toward the ceiling, steady state merely converts exponential approach to asymptotic. The ceiling is still there.

**Zero production (C = 0):** Theoretically, Σ would decay exponentially. At δ ≈ 2%/year, the stock halves in ~35 years. After a decade of zero production, >80% of original P persists. In practice, zero production means cascading failure—no food, water, electricity. Historical collapses (Roman Empire, Black Death, Soviet dissolution) saw energy fall because structure was dismantled or depopulated, not by choice.

**Sustained 50% GDP cut for a decade:** Rough estimate: W would fall ~17%. Power demand would fall ~17%. After a decade of depression worse than any in history, we'd still demand >80% of current energy.

**Bottom line:** Policy operates on the flow Y (annual output). The constraint operates on the stock Σ (assembly stock). The mismatch is mathematical.

### Temporal batteries

Fossil fuels and fissile materials are *temporal batteries*: exergy stored over geological timescales. Their strategic significance is timing. A civilisation that exhausts them during uncontrolled growth has committed the resulting Σ—it exists, demands maintenance, and cannot be wished away. Conversely, deploying them for space-industrial bootstrap or coordination institutions retains manoeuvrability. Burning them for undirected growth is thermodynamic seed corn.

### The decay spectrum

Different components decay at different rates: Roman aqueducts (millennia), industrial plant (20–40 years), servers (3–7 years), smartphones (3 years). As civilisation shifts toward higher-turnover components (digital, services), the weighted δ rises. More of each year's construction goes to replacement, less to net accumulation. The system must run faster to stay in place. Tainter's diminishing returns to complexity: more sophisticated structure demands more maintenance per unit of stock. A high-δ civilisation is more fragile, not more flexible.

### Carbon lock-in is assembly lock-in

"Carbon lock-in" (Unruh 2000)—fossil infrastructure operating for its full lifetime—is a symptom. The underlying condition: every structure added to Σ creates a maintenance obligation that persists. Even complete decarbonisation doesn't reduce P. P is coupled to Σ, not to carbon intensity. The structure built by fossil fuels demands the same wattage whether watts come from coal or fission. Solving the carbon problem without solving the growth problem trades a near-term greenhouse crisis for a longer-term waste heat crisis.

### Time constants vs control timescales

- **Assembly stock:** $\tau_\delta = 1/\delta \approx 50$–100 years ($\delta$ = decay rate)
- **Wealth integral:** $\tau \approx W/Y \approx 43$ years ($W$ = wealth, $Y$ = output)
- **Atmospheric CO₂:** centuries
- **Ocean thermal mass:** decades to centuries

**Control timescales:** Parliamentary term 4–5 years. Corporate planning 5–10 years. Paris targets ~25 years. All shorter than τ. Policy modulates the flow. It cannot rapidly alter the integral.

**Energy transitions:** Smil documents wood→coal, coal→oil, oil→gas each took 50–70 years for majority share. The question isn't just whether clean energy can replace fossil (it may). It's whether it can replace *total* throughput demanded by exponentially growing W (almost certainly not, absent changes to growth dynamics).

### The double lock

Ratchet + inertia: the trajectory aims at the ceiling (competitive selection), and cannot turn quickly (stock responds to flow with decades of lag). Together they describe a system whose behaviour is largely determined by its accumulated past.

## 3.3 The Amplifiers: Money, Debt, and Governance

### Everything here is human convention

This section depends on *nothing* from physics. Debt has been forgiven throughout history. Monetary systems have been adopted and abandoned. The gold standard, Bretton Woods—all human inventions. They can, in principle, be reformed.

**What the amplifiers do:** They don't create the trajectory. The ratchet would drive us toward the ceiling even without them. They *accelerate* the approach. They convert a physical tendency into a compulsion.

### The temporal pump: endogenous money creation

Banks don't intermediate between savers and borrowers. When a bank makes a loan, it *creates* new money (Bank of England 2014). The borrower gets purchasing power that didn't exist. That power commands labour and materials—exergy—to build new structure. Each loan adds to $\Sigma$ and commits future power $\Delta P = \Gamma\cdot\Delta\Sigma$.

**The obligation:** The borrower must repay $L(1+r)^n$ ($L$ = loan principal, $r$ = interest rate, $n$ = maturity in years). Repayment requires future economic output Y, which requires energy. Credit creation *pulls exergy from the future into the present*. It's extraordinarily useful—it finances long-gestation projects no one could fund from savings. But each loan bets that the future will be larger, in energetic terms, than the present. When that bet fails at scale, the result is thermodynamic: claims on energy that doesn't exist.

**Soddy (1926):** "Debts are subject to the laws of mathematics rather than physics. Unlike wealth, which decays, debts grow at so much per cent per annum." Real wealth (Σ, assembly stock) decays at δ (decay rate). Virtual wealth (debt) has no friction. The two are yoked by the assumption that claims can always be redeemed against physical assets. When they can't: inflation, default, or crisis.

### The growth floor: compound interest

Debt grows: $\frac{dD}{dt} = r \cdot D$ ($r$ = real interest rate, $D$ = debt stock). At 3% real interest, doubling time ~23 years. For solvency, the physical economy must grow at least as fast as financial claims:

$$Y \geq (r + \delta) \cdot W$$

($Y$ = output, $r$ = interest rate, $\delta$ = decay rate, $W$ = wealth). Output must cover both debt service and maintenance of existing stock.

**Critical distinction:** δ (the decay rate) is Tier 1 (physics). No one can repeal the Second Law. r (the interest rate) is Tier 3 (convention). Debt can be forgiven. Interest can be zero (Japan) or negative (ECB). The solvency condition nests a reformable convention inside an immutable law.

**Consequences:** (1) Growth floor on energy consumption, independent of technology or preference. (2) The imperative accelerates with scale—both δW (maintenance obligation) and rD (debt service) grow with their stocks. (3) Timescale mismatch: debt maturities are years; physical system responds over decades. The financial system can generate claims faster than the physical system can honour them.

### The Minsky correction

Hyman Minsky: credit markets cycle through hedge finance (income covers interest and principal), speculative finance (income covers interest, principal refinanced), and Ponzi finance (neither covered, reliance on asset appreciation). The Minsky moment: when the physical system can no longer supply the energy flow required by financial commitments. Virtual wealth snaps back to real wealth. Claims are destroyed until debt-to-wealth returns to a supportable level.

**2008:** A localised demonstration. Global debt-to-GDP ~350% by 2024. Sustainable only with continued growth. Continued growth requires continued energy growth → waste heat → tighter temperature constraint. Relaxing the debt constraint requires growth that accelerates toward the ceiling. Constraining power requires steady state, which is structurally incompatible with debt service under current architecture.

**Key point:** The Minsky cycle is a property of a specific financial architecture. A different one would produce different dynamics. But the trajectory would exist without it. Debt makes the problem worse and faster. It doesn't create it.

### The governance frequency mismatch

**Physical system:** τ ≈ 50–100 years. CO₂ residence: centuries. Ocean equilibration: decades to centuries.

**Political system:** Electoral cycles 2–5 years. Finance minister tenure <3 years. Corporate planning 5–10 years. Debt maturities months to 30 years.

**Result:** The control vector oscillates on a 2–5 year cycle. The system it's steering responds on 50–100 year timescales. A controller that oscillates faster than the plant's response produces no net effect (oscillations average out) or destabilising resonance. Successive governments cancel each other's carbon pricing. The physical system barely registers.

**Example:** Australian carbon price 2012–2014. Introduced, operated two years, repealed. Atmospheric CO₂: 394 → 399 ppm. No detectable effect.

**Monetary policy:** Central banks target inflation and employment on 1–3 year horizons. Each rate adjustment modulates energy dissipation (lower rates → more borrowing → more construction → more Σ). But the "thermometer" reads CPI and unemployment, not the physical variables that determine viability.

**Fiscal policy** can redirect the *composition* of P (power throughput; e.g. carbon tax, renewable investment). It cannot easily change the *magnitude*, because that's set by Σ (assembly stock) through Γ (power–assembly coupling), which hasn't declined over fifty years despite dramatic changes in energy mix and economic structure.

### The empirical signature: Garrett and Hanley

Garrett's $P \approx \lambda W$ held ~constant 1970–2019 across information revolution, China's rise, Soviet collapse, 2008 crisis, shale gas, renewables. The financial architecture enforces the ratio: when P falls below debt-service needs, Minsky correction; when P rises above, credit creation raises Σ and re-establishes the ratio at higher level. The system oscillates around the attractor.

**Hanley (2025):** The ratio *diverged* before ~1970. It converged to current values around the Bretton Woods collapse (1971). That's when the global financial system became integrated enough to enforce a uniform growth compulsion worldwide. Removing the gold constraint let endogenous money operate at full scale. From then on, the financial amplifier was fully engaged. The composite Γ stabilised not because physics changed, but because institutions locked the components into a self-reinforcing regime.

**Refinement:** Declining EROI (energy return on investment) means the system may run faster on the gross energy treadmill to maintain the same net output. The effective coupling may be tightening even as the gross ratio holds steady.

### What the amplifiers do and do not explain

They convert *tendency* (maximum power) into *compulsion* (must grow or face financial collapse). Removing them would slow the trajectory, widen the viability kernel, buy time. It would not buy escape. The predicament is created by the Second Law, finite radiative surface, and competitive dynamics. The amplifiers make it worse and faster. The physics makes it real.

**The critical distinction:** Institutional reform has ample historical precedent. Repealing the Second Law has none.
